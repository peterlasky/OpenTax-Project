# P535

Source files:

- local repo file: `forms-instructions-and-publications/publications/p535.pdf`
- fallback substantive source used for summary: IRS prior-year `Publication 535 (2022) - Business Expenses`

Important file-status note:

- the local repo `p535.pdf` is not an actual PDF
- it is an IRS HTML page saved with a `.pdf` extension
- that page says `Publication 535` was discontinued after the `2022` edition
- so for substantive rules, use the real `2022` publication plus the IRS replacement-resource map

What the local HTML page contributes:

- Publication 535 is no longer maintained as a current-year publication
- topic routing now points to other publications/forms instead of a single master business-expense book
- especially relevant successor resources:

Why this publication still matters for the tax program:

- it is still the best compact map of what counts as a business expense vs capitalized cost
- it identifies which expenses belong on `Schedule C`, `Schedule E`, `Schedule F`, interest-limitation forms, depreciation forms, and amortization paths
- it is useful for schema design even when current-year details must come from successor forms/instructions

Core structure of Publication 535 (2022):

- Chapter 1: Deducting Business Expenses
- Chapter 2: Employees' Pay
- Chapter 3: Rent Expense
- Chapter 4: Interest
- Chapter 5: Taxes
- Chapter 6: Insurance
- Chapter 7: Costs You Can Deduct or Capitalize
- Chapter 8: Amortization
- Chapter 9: Depletion
- Chapter 10: Business Bad Debts
- Chapter 11: Other Expenses

Global rules to preserve in the model:

- an expense is deductible only if it is:
  - `ordinary`
  - `necessary`
  - directly connected to carrying on a trade or business
- an expense is not currently deductible if it must be:
  - capitalized into basis
  - recovered through depreciation
  - recovered through amortization
  - recovered through depletion
  - included in inventory / cost of goods sold
- timing matters:
  - cash vs accrual accounting rules affect when an expense is deductible
  - prepaid amounts often cannot be deducted all at once

Chapter 1: Deducting business expenses

- baseline distinction:
  - current deductible business expense
  - capital expense
  - personal expense
- business-use allocation is required for mixed-use items
- common deductible categories include:
  - advertising
  - supplies
  - wages
  - rent
  - utilities
  - travel
  - certain professional fees
- personal, living, or family expenses are not deductible unless a specific rule allows partial business treatment
- home-office, vehicle, and travel-type categories need separate substantiation / special rules rather than generic deduction logic

Chapter 2: Employees' pay

- wages, salaries, commissions, bonuses, vacation pay, sick pay, and some fringe-related costs can be deductible compensation
- payments to employees are generally compensation, not gifts
- reimbursement treatment depends on whether the plan is:
  - accountable plan
  - nonaccountable plan
- this affects whether the amount is deducted as the underlying expense or as wages
- meals furnished to employees:
  - often only `50%` deductible
  - some special cases allow `100%`
- employee achievement awards have detailed property / value limitations and are not a generic cash-equivalent deduction bucket
- COVID-era sick/family leave and retention-credit discussion is historical and should mostly be treated as expired-path reference material

Model implications from employee-pay chapter:

- reimbursement flows need a policy flag for accountable vs nonaccountable treatment
- employee meals should not be merged blindly with all meal deductions
- some “employee benefit” deductions belong in payroll/employer logic, not individual-return logic

Chapter 3: Rent expense

- rent is generally deductible if paid for business property the taxpayer does not own
- if a payment creates a long-term benefit or ownership-like interest, capitalization may be required
- lease-related items can include:
  - rent
  - taxes on leased property
  - leasehold improvement considerations
- advance rent generally is not simply “deduct when paid” if it applies to future periods

Chapter 4: Interest

- strong distinction between deductible business interest and nondeductible / capitalized interest
- interest must be allocated based on how the borrowed funds are actually used
- major subtopics:
  - business interest expense limitation
  - interest allocation
  - interest you can deduct
  - interest you cannot deduct
  - capitalization of interest
- personal interest is generally nondeductible
- interest on income tax is generally nondeductible
- some mortgage interest belongs in itemized-deduction logic, not business-expense logic
- below-market loans / gift loans can create deemed-interest consequences
- `section 163(j)` business-interest limitation is a separate computation path; do not model all business interest as automatically deductible

Model implications from interest chapter:

- loan proceeds need “use of funds” classification
- interest may route to:
  - Schedule C
  - Schedule E
  - Form 4952
  - Form 8990
  - basis/capitalization
- home mortgage interest and investment interest should stay out of generic business-interest buckets

Chapter 5: Taxes

- some taxes paid in a trade or business are deductible
- federal income taxes are generally not a business deduction
- employment taxes, real estate taxes, and certain excise taxes may be deductible depending on use/context
- self-employment tax is not a generic business expense; its deduction path is special
- state and local taxes may belong in different places depending on business/entity/property context

Model implications from taxes chapter:

- distinguish deductible business taxes from:
  - federal income taxes
  - penalties
  - taxes added to basis
- some taxes route to Schedule C/E/F, some to Schedule A, some are nondeductible

Chapter 6: Insurance

- ordinary business insurance can be deductible
- category matters:
  - liability
  - malpractice
  - workers' compensation
  - fire/theft/flood/storm
  - business interruption
  - vehicle insurance (business-use allocated)
- premiums on some life insurance arrangements are nondeductible, especially where the taxpayer is directly or indirectly a beneficiary
- self-employed health insurance now points operationally to `Form 7206`

Chapter 7: Costs you can deduct or capitalize

- one of the most important modeling chapters
- many costs are not inherently deductible or capital; treatment depends on facts
- startup, acquisition, improvement, production, and facilitative costs may need capitalization
- repair vs improvement distinction is critical
- inventory / production costs may fall under capitalization rules
- research and experimental expenditures changed beginning in 2022:
  - generally no immediate full deduction election
  - generally capitalize and amortize instead

Model implications from chapter 7:

- add explicit “capitalize vs deduct now” decision points for ambiguous costs
- repairs/maintenance should be distinct from improvements
- research expenses need current-law capitalization/amortization treatment, not old immediate-expense treatment

Chapter 8: Amortization

- costs with multi-year benefit may be amortized rather than deducted immediately
- includes research / experimental cost amortization after the law change
- foreign research generally uses longer amortization than domestic research
- some intangibles and startup-type items have specialized amortization rules

Model implications from amortization chapter:

- create separate amortizable-asset / deferred-cost structures rather than flattening into “other expenses”
- domestic vs foreign research needs separate treatment

Chapter 9: Depletion

- depletion applies to extractive-resource contexts
- not relevant to most ordinary individual returns, but still important for certain businesses / royalties / mineral interests
- separate from depreciation and amortization

Chapter 10: Business bad debts

- business bad debts are different from nonbusiness bad debts
- classification matters because tax treatment differs substantially
- business bad debts can be ordinary deductions if properly connected to the trade or business
- must have bona fide debt, not just failed income expectation or equity investment

Model implications from bad-debt chapter:

- do not allow generic “write-off” entries without classifying:
  - bona fide debt
  - business vs nonbusiness
  - partially worthless vs wholly worthless

Chapter 11: Other expenses

- catch-all chapter for important special categories
- includes practical high-frequency topics like:
  - car and truck expenses
  - travel
  - meals
  - gifts
  - education
  - subscriptions / dues / professional fees
  - some utilities / communications
- standard mileage rates were highlighted for 2022:
  - `58.5 cents` through `2022-06-30`
  - `62.5 cents` from `2022-07-01` through `2022-12-31`
- many of these categories have substantiation limits and should not be modeled as unrestricted free-form deductions

High-value routing map for the tax engine:

- `vehicle`: actual-expense vs mileage method, with business-use allocation
- `travel/meals`: substantiation-sensitive, often 50% limit for meals
- `interest`: possibly Schedule C / E / 4952 / 8990 / capitalize
- `depreciation`: `Form 4562` / `Pub. 946`
- `business-use home`: `Pub. 587`
- `passive / at-risk`: `Form 8582` / `Form 6198`
- `investment interest`: `Form 4952`
- `QBI`: `Form 8995` / `8995-A`
- `self-employed health insurance`: `Form 7206`

What should be treated as stale or historical:

- the publication itself is final at `2022`
- COVID-era employer credits discussed there are largely expired
- mileage numbers in the publication are historical, not reusable for 2025
- use it for rule structure and topic routing, but cross-check current-year thresholds and percentages elsewhere

Bottom-line build guidance:

- use Pub. 535 as a taxonomy and rule-shaping source
- use successor publications/forms for live current-law computations
- never lump all business write-offs into a single “other expense” bucket
- preserve these distinctions in the schema:
  - currently deductible
  - capitalized
  - depreciated
  - amortized
  - depleted
  - limited
  - nondeductible
