# P570

Source file: `forms-instructions-and-publications/publications/p570.pdf`

Title: `Publication 570 - Tax Guide for Individuals With Income From U.S. Territories`

Tax year focus:

- for preparing `2025` returns
- due date called out: `April 15, 2026`

Territories covered:

- American Samoa
- Puerto Rico
- CNMI
- Guam
- USVI

Core structure of the publication:

- Chapter 1: who is a `bona fide resident`
- Chapter 2: whether income is `territory-source` or `effectively connected` with a territory trade or business
- Chapter 3: which return(s) must be filed
- Chapter 4: how to complete the U.S. return when a U.S. return is required
- Chapter 5: how to get help / territory contacts

2025 items worth modeling:

- standard deduction increased for all filers
- self-employment social security wage base: `176,100`
- optional methods max income: `7,240`
- digital asset question must be answered; do not leave blank
- filing-status label `qualifying widow(er)` is now `qualifying surviving spouse`
- Puerto Rico ACTC rule changed: bona fide Puerto Rico residents no longer need `3+` qualifying children; `1+` qualifying child may be enough
- deductions tied to tips, overtime, passenger vehicle loan interest, and the enhanced senior deduction may be disallowed or reduced when territory income is excluded; cross-check Schedule `1-A`

High-value filing logic:

- a taxpayer may need:
  - U.S. return only
  - territory return only
  - both returns
- this mostly turns on:
  - bona fide residence status
  - source of income
  - whether income is excluded from U.S. tax
- if not a bona fide resident, or otherwise required to file a U.S. return, use the U.S. return completion rules in chapter 4

Bona fide residence notes:

- residence status is foundational; build it early in the Federal Information Worksheet
- if a taxpayer begins or ends bona fide residence in a U.S. territory, check `Form 8898`
- active-duty U.S. Armed Forces members whose legal residence is Puerto Rico or American Samoa are treated under special rules and may have dual filing obligations

Income sourcing rules to preserve in the model:

- wages / compensation: source where services are performed
- pension contributions portion: source follows where services were performed
- pension investment earnings: source where pension trust is located
- interest: source by payer residence
- dividends: source by where corporation is created / organized
- rents: source by location of property
- natural-resource royalties: source by location of property
- patent / copyright / similar royalties: source by where property is used
- inventory sales: source generally tied to where sold

Important tax overlays:

- self-employment tax usually still goes to the United States for bona fide territory residents with SE income
- Additional Medicare Tax may still apply
- NIIT:
  - potentially relevant to bona fide Puerto Rico and American Samoa residents who have a U.S. filing obligation and enough non-territory income
  - generally not directly applicable to CNMI / Guam / USVI bona fide residents because they generally do not have a federal filing obligation
- NIIT should still be considered in estimated-tax logic where applicable

Credit / benefit notes:

- EIC generally cannot be claimed on the U.S. return by a bona fide territory resident
- to claim EIC on a U.S. return, home must have been in the `50 states or D.C.` for more than half the year
- for a child-based EIC claim, the child must also have lived with the taxpayer in the `50 states or D.C.` for more than half the year
- foreign financial asset reporting may require `Form 8938`, but CNMI / Guam / USVI residents generally do not have that IRS filing if they have no U.S. return filing requirement

Forms explicitly relevant:

- `1040-SS`
- `1040`
- `1116`
- `4563`
- `4868`
- `5074`
- `8898`
- `8938`
- `W-7`
- `8822`

Model-building implications:

- build a strong `territory residence / bona fide residence` fact set first
- keep territory sourcing separate from federal sourcing assumptions used for ordinary domestic returns
- do not assume federal filing just because there is income; territory filing can replace or coexist depending on status
- Puerto Rico ACTC logic needs a dedicated branch
- NIIT and Additional Medicare Tax need territory-sensitive gating
- EIC gating must treat the territories differently from the 50 states / D.C.

Short caution list:

- territory rules are not just a deduction or credit overlay; they can reroute the entire filing path
- `Puerto Rico` and `American Samoa` often behave differently from `CNMI`, `Guam`, and `USVI`
- when the publication says to contact the territory tax department, that usually means mirrored or territory-administered rules should not be silently treated as ordinary IRS-only logic
