# P502

Source file: `forms-instructions-and-publications/publications/p502.pdf`

Title: `Publication 502 - Medical and Dental Expenses`

Role in the tax program:

- primary rule source for Schedule A medical deduction
- useful for deciding:
  - what counts as deductible medical expense
  - whose expenses can be included
  - how reimbursements reduce the deduction
  - what threshold applies against AGI

Core deduction rule:

- medical and dental expenses are itemized on `Schedule A`
- deductible amount is only the portion above `7.5%` of `AGI`

High-value build rules:

- deductible medical expense must be primarily for diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or to affect a body function
- general-health spending is not enough
- classic nonqualifying examples include things that are merely good for general health

What generally counts:

- doctor / dentist / practitioner payments
- equipment / supplies / diagnostic devices
- transportation for medical care
- insurance premiums for medical care
- qualified long-term care services
- limited qualified long-term care insurance amounts

What generally does not count:

- expenses reimbursed by insurance or another source
- future-year care paid outside the allowed timing rules
- general-health items with no disease/disability treatment purpose

Timing rules:

- include expenses paid in the current year
- credit-card charge counts in the year charged, not when the card is later paid
- mailed check generally counts when mailed / delivered
- missed prior-year deductible expense generally belongs on `Form 1040-X`, not the current-year return

Whose expenses can be included:

- taxpayer
- spouse
- dependents
- special rules for decedents and multiple support agreements
- for spouse/dependent expenses, timing of marriage/dependency relative to care/payment matters

Dependency implications:

- medical-expense inclusion rules are not identical to all ordinary dependency rules, but they heavily rely on dependent status concepts
- publication explicitly points back to dependent concepts and special exceptions
- community-property and separate-return payment allocation rules matter

Separate return / community property implications:

- on separate returns in non-community-property states, each spouse generally deducts only what that spouse actually paid
- joint-account payments are generally split equally unless facts show otherwise
- community-property states generally split community-funded medical expenses equally
- Pub. `555` cross-reference matters here

Medical-expense definition implications for schema design:

- do not use one giant “medical expenses” number only
- better categories:
  - insurance premiums
  - provider payments
  - transportation
  - long-term care
  - medical equipment/supplies
  - reimbursements received
- reimbursements should reduce the deductible pool, not be treated as separate deductions

Interaction notes:

- self-employed health insurance is discussed, but that path is not just a Schedule A itemized deduction; keep self-employed health insurance logic distinct from ordinary medical itemization
- premium tax credit / `Form 8962` can interact with insurance premium treatment
- `Pub. 969` cross-reference matters for HSAs and related health-favored plans

Model-building takeaways:

- keep Schedule A medical logic as:
  - qualifying expenses
  - minus reimbursements
  - minus AGI floor (`7.5%`)
- preserve who-paid / who-benefited facts where possible
- separate medical itemized deduction logic from self-employed-health-insurance and HSA logic
